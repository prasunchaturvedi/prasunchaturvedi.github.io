One Year of the DPDP Rules: Is Odisha's Public Sector Ready for May 2027?

25 November 2026 · Prasun Chaturvedi

The first year is over

The Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Rules completed one year this month. Notified on 13 November 2025, they gave every organisation handling Indian citizens' digital personal data 18 months to get ready. Six months are left.

Most of the commentary has been written for private companies. But some of the largest holders of personal data in Odisha are the government departments, directorates, urban local bodies, universities, recruitment boards and public sector undertakings. Between them, they run the portals through which citizens apply for jobs, scholarships, pensions, certificates and welfare benefits.

The question for them is no longer whether the law applies. It is whether they will be ready by May 2027.

Where the clock stands

The Rules come into force in three phases. The hard deadline is May 2027.

PhaseIn force fromWhat it covers
1November 2025Definitions and the Data Protection Board (Rules 1, 2, 17–21)
2November 2026Registration of Consent Managers (Rule 4)
3May 2027The core duties: notice, consent, security safeguards, breach intimation, retention and erasure, children's data, grievance redress (Rules 3, 5–16, 22, 23)

Two points matter for planners. First, a proposal reported in January 2026 to compress the window to 12 months has not been notified, so May 2027 stands for now. Second, the window can still be shortened, so planning for the last minute is a risk for concerned departments.

Some governments have already started action. Delhi's IT Department, by an office memorandum of 3 July 2026, directed every department, local body, board and corporation under it to review its portals, databases, processes and contracts that touch personal data, and to treat compliance as a priority. Others need to move fast.

Why government is not exempt by default

A common assumption in public offices is that the Act exempts government. That is only partially true.

The Act lets the State process personal data without fresh consent when it delivers a subsidy, benefit, service, certificate, licence or permit. That is a "legitimate use". But this route comes with conditions. Processing must follow the standards in the Rules, and the department remains a Data Fiduciary: it must still protect the data, report breaches and answer grievances.

The wider exemptions apply only to instrumentalities the Central Government specifically notifies, for named purposes such as sovereignty, security or public order. A district office running a scholarship portal, a university running admissions or a Commission running a recruitment drive should assume the full duties apply unless a notification says otherwise.

Outsourcing does not move the duty either. When a vendor hosts the portal, the department is still accountable. The vendor is a Data Processor acting on its behalf, and that relationship has to be governed by contract.

Odisha's exposure is growing

Odisha is digitising faster than most states, and every new system adds personal data to protect.

  • OSWAS 3.0. In August 2026 the State Cabinet approved the next version of the Odisha Secretariat Workflow Automation System, reported at about Rs 122.6 crore, extending paperless working down to block and tehsil offices. Files at that level carry names, addresses, land records, grievances and identity documents.
  • Cyber security investment. The State Budget 2026–27 provides Rs 89 crore for a Centre of Excellence for Cyber Security to strengthen police capacity in cyber monitoring, digital forensics and response.
  • Citizen-facing portals. Recruitment, scholarship, welfare, health and land services already hold the data of many millions of residents.

The investment in enforcement is welcome. The gap is on the other side: the everyday data hygiene of the offices that collect the data in the first place.

Some bodies are, of course, more mature than others. In recruitment drives, I have seen public bodies issue corrigendum after corrigendum to their application forms, collecting more applicant data as requirements evolve. The result is a fragmented, hard-to-manage dataset. Rather than fix it, the instinct is often to hand all of it, biometrics, signatures and photographs included, to an outsourced service provider and be done with it. Such organisations need guidance on what the DPDP regime expects: what to share, with whom, and how.

Other departments have internalised privacy and confidentiality in their daily work. That maturity comes from two things: relentless training of every employee on why data privacy matters, and a governance structure designed around it.

A ten-point readiness checklist

Each item can be started this quarter without new legislation or a large budget.

  1. Name an owner. Appoint a senior officer accountable for personal data in each department, with a published contact for citizens.
  2. Map the data. List every portal, database, spreadsheet and paper-to-digital workflow that holds personal data, with its purpose and who can access it.
  3. Collect less. Drop fields that the service does not need. A full Aadhaar number collected "just in case" is a liability, not an asset.
  4. Rewrite the notices. Tell citizens in plain Odia and English what is collected, why, for how long, and how to complain.
  5. Fix retention. Set a deletion date for each dataset. Recruitment and admission data, in particular, is often kept for years after its purpose ends.
  6. Review vendor contracts. Add clauses on security safeguards, breach reporting, sub-contracting, audit rights and return or deletion of data at contract end.
  7. Harden access. Enforce multi-factor login for officials and vendors, remove shared accounts and log who viewed what.
  8. Rehearse a breach. Agree who informs the Data Protection Board and the affected citizens, and run one tabletop exercise before May 2027.
  9. Open a grievance channel. Give citizens a way to ask what is held about them and to have errors corrected, with a time limit for replies.
  10. Train the front line. Clerks, data-entry operators and contract staff handle most of the data. A one-hour session on what not to share on WhatsApp prevents many incidents.

In one such department, officials were ready to hand over their data files on a USB stick. We sat down together and agreed on exactly which data the work actually required. We then set up a secure channel for the exchange, with clear rules on where the data would reside and when it would be destroyed once its purpose was served. The change met real resistance: a thin team saw the caution as overhead rather than as a sign of process maturity. By the end, though, the department had built data protection into its working culture instead of treating it as a burden.

Compliance is a trust dividend

It is tempting to treat the DPDP Rules as another file to close before a deadline. That misses the opportunity. Citizens share their data with the State because they must. Treating that data carefully is how public institutions earn the trust that digital governance depends on.

Odisha has shown it can move fast on digital administration. The next six months are a chance to show it can also lead on protecting the people that administration serves. Departments that start the checklist now will reach May 2027 with working systems, not emergency circulars.
